Needs. Wants. Savings. Bullshit.

The first three you already know. The fourth is the one that actually changes behaviour — because “Wants” is a category you can rationalize, and this one isn't. Give it 5% of your paycheck and watch how fast you notice the overdraft fees.

It knows the difference between overspending and running out.

A month can close in the black and still bounce twice, because the rent landed before the paycheck did. Cense walks the month day by day — using the days your regulars are due — and tells you where the floor is.

On current timing you go below zero on the 11th, bottoming out at −$273.24. The month still ends at $3,743.76 — this is a timing problem, not an overspending one, and it is what overdraft fees are made of.

It says what the debt costs you if you don't outrun it.

Add the interest rate and Cense tells you how long it takes at your current payment, what the interest costs on the way, and what another $50 a month would save. When the payment is smaller than the monthly interest, it says so outright.

At $250.00 a month this never gets paid off — the interest at 180% is $300.00 a month, more than the payment. It grows from here.

Point it at a bank statement and it does the filing.

Cense works out which columns hold what, skips your deposits, proposes a bucket for every line, and skips charges you already have — so re-importing the same statement doesn't double your month. The file is read inside the page.

Find out where it actually went.

Three minutes to set up: tell it what you make, add the charges you already know are coming, then log what you spend. About thirty seconds a day after that, which is the only budgeting habit anyone actually keeps.

Open Cense

The honest part: Cense keeps your data in your own browser, which is what makes it private — and also means it doesn't sync between your laptop and your phone, and clearing your browser data will erase it. There's an Export button in Settings. Use it now and then.
